Design brief — v1 — Sept 2026

Clocktower by Post

An async way to play Blood on the Clock Tower across a wedding reception — so people can plot, deduce, and still mingle.

01

The idea

You and your friends play Blood on the Clock Tower in person. At the reception you want to play and circulate at the same time. This app lets the game breathe across the day: people talk in person, the clock ticks for everyone at once, and the group only physically reconvenes for the big beats — a death, an execution, a nomination round.

The Storyteller runs everything from one screen: they pre-plan the night, release information when each phase turns, and field private requests to meet. Everyone else carries a phone that tells them whose neighbour they are, what they learned last night, and when to gather.

Hard constraints

  • No in-app messaging. Lying and reading people stays face-to-face. The app never becomes a chat client.
  • One synced clock. Every phone agrees on the phase and the countdown, even on bad venue wifi.
  • Trivial to join. Open a link, type your name. No install, no account, no app store.
  • Storyteller does less, not more. Pre-planning up front so the day itself is mostly releasing what's already written.
02

Decisions locked in

Done in person — the dramatic ritual stays real. The app records who was nominated, the vote counts, and the deaths. It also gives each player a private “prime suspect” tracker and notes.
The clock

Character art
Placeholders for now — ability text and neutral role icons. Revisit real token art near the end of the build.

You know Python and a little of other languages, new to app development. I choose the stack and lead the build; you test, play, and give feedback.
03

The stack

The app is a Progressive Web App: a website that installs to the home screen and runs full-screen like a native app, from a single URL. Nothing to compile or distribute.

LayerChoiceWhy
Front endSvelteKitSmall, readable components; the best PWA and offline story of the mainstream frameworks; gentle for someone coming from Python.
Backend & dataSupabaseHosted Postgres + realtime subscriptions + simple auth. No server for you to run or scale. Free tier covers a wedding many times over.
RealtimeSupabase RealtimePhase changes, the meet queue, and the gather signal push to every phone over one websocket.
HostingVercel or NetlifyDeploy on every git push. Free. The app is served from a CDN and cached on-device, so it loads even when the venue wifi is coughing.
Game logicPlain TypeScript modulesScript definitions, night order, and the “what does this role need” rules are small pure functions — they read a lot like Python and are easy to unit-test.

Why not a Python server

You could write this with FastAPI or NiceGUI and stay in Python. The problem is the venue: a server-rendered app shows a blank screen the moment a phone drops connection, and you'd be running and babysitting that server on the day. Supabase + a cached PWA degrades gracefully instead — a 30-second dropout desyncs nobody. The bit of TypeScript you take on is worth that trade.

04

The synced clock

This is the part that has to be right. The server never streams a ticking number. It stores one fact:

phase = "night 2"  ·  started_at = 19:42:10  ·  duration = 12 min  ·  paused = false

Each phone computes its own countdown from those values against its own clock. A brief disconnect changes nothing — the phone already knows when night 2 ends. When the Storyteller extends or pauses, that's a tiny write that every phone picks up on reconnect. Only the Storyteller's device needs a reliable connection, and even theirs can lag without breaking anyone else.

Clock drift between phones is handled with a one-time offset check against the server on join, so a phone whose clock is two minutes fast still shows the right countdown.

06

Data model sketch

Roughly the tables Supabase will hold:

  • scripts — id, name, author, min / max players, the ordered character list with team tags, first-night and other-night order, and setup modifiers (Baron: +2 Outsiders, and so on).
  • games — join code, script id, current phase, phase started_at / duration / paused, gather signal state, the team composition in use (computed from the script + count, Storyteller-overridable).
  • seats — game id, seat index (the circle order), player name, assigned character, alive / dead, ghost vote used.
  • grimoire — Storyteller-only: real character per seat, status tokens (poisoned, protected, red herring, drunk), reminder notes.
  • night_actions — game id, night number, seat id, character, prompt shown, choice(s) made, result text, released_at.
  • prep_notes — Storyteller drafts keyed by night + seat, with a released flag.
  • meet_requests — seat id, reason, created_at, status (waiting / met / dismissed).
  • day_log — nominations, vote tallies, executions, deaths — one row per event.
  • player_notes — seat id, free text, prime-suspect seat id. Locked to that player by row-level security.

Supabase row-level security is what keeps a player's phone from ever reading the grimoire or another player's notes — it's enforced at the database, not just hidden in the UI.

07

The circle & neighbours

Players are seated in a fixed order at the start — the Storyteller arranges the circle by dragging names. Physically people wander off, but their seat stays put, so “neighbour” abilities still work.

Each player's screen shows the full circle with their own seat highlighted and their two living neighbours marked. When someone dies, the app recomputes living neighbours automatically — the Empath on night 3 sees the right two people without the Storyteller doing arithmetic. Dead players stay in the circle as ghosts so the geometry never shifts.

Above 15, and below 5

  • Travellers extend any script past 15 — add up to 5 for a practical ceiling around 20. Each Traveller is a public role, aligned good or evil by the Storyteller, and sits in the circle on top of the normal composition. A few groups run larger than 20, but 20 is the recommended max.
  • Fabled are Storyteller-side helper characters (they fix balance problems or add house rules). They live in the grimoire, not the circle, and don't count toward the player total.
  • Teensyville is the format for tiny groups: purpose-built 5–7 player scripts with their own composition rules. Trouble Brewing technically runs at 5–6 but is flatter there; a dedicated teensy script plays better.
  • Custom scripts from the community database each declare their own character list and supported range. The app treats them exactly like the official three — a data file with a min and max.

For the wedding: pick the script once you know roughly how many guests will play. If it's a big, fluid crowd, Trouble Brewing plus Travellers is the safe choice; the app should let the Storyteller add and align Travellers live as people opt in or drop out.

10

Storyteller tools

Prep board

Before the day, the Storyteller lays out each night: for every seat, a draft of what they'll learn and any nudge or advice. Drafts sit unreleased. When night falls, the Storyteller walks the night order and releases each one — or edits it first if the game has moved.

Meet queue

A player taps “request to meet” and adds a one-line reason. It joins an ordered list on the Storyteller's screen with a timestamp. The Storyteller marks each as met or dismissed; the player sees their place in line so they know whether to keep mingling or hover nearby.

Gather signal

One button on the Storyteller's screen rings and buzzes every phone at once, with an optional short reason. This is the “stop mingling, come back to the courtyard” call — for an execution, a dramatic death, or a whole-group story beat.

11

Player tools

  • Circle view — the seating ring, your seat, your two living neighbours, who's dead.
  • Tonight / today — the current phase, the countdown, and whatever the Storyteller has released to you.
  • Notebook — private free-text notes plus a prime-suspect marker you can point at a seat. Yours alone.
  • History — the public record: who was nominated, vote counts, who died and when. No private information, same for everyone.
  • Request to meet — the button and your position in the queue.

Risks & unknowns

RiskPlan
Venue connectivityThe single biggest one. Cached PWA + the offset-clock pattern. Rehearse at the actual venue if possible. Consider a phone hotspot as Storyteller backup.
People drift too far / forget the gameThe gather signal and a gentle phase-change buzz. Keep phases long enough to mingle, short enough to stay engaged — tune in playtests.
Storyteller overload on the dayThe prep board is the mitigation. If the Storyteller is still typing during the night, the design has failed — test that.
Wedding-day chaos in generalThe app must survive someone joining late, a dead battery, a player leaving for photos. Every screen needs a “you're behind, here's the current state” recovery.
IPPrivate use only. No public deployment, no fees, no official art or text shipped in the repo.
13

Limits of this model

Being straight about what this design and I can and can't do, so there are no surprises in month eight.

This is scaffolding, not a rules engine

Blood on the Clock Tower is deliberately adjudicated by a human. Registering as good or evil, how poison or drunkenness bends a result, jinxes between specific characters, “the Storyteller decides” — these resist automation. The app models who wakes, in what order, and what to ask them, and computes a suggested answer only where it's safe (Chef, Empath, Undertaker). Everything else the Storyteller confirms or overrides. A fully automatic resolver is not a goal, and I'd distrust one that claimed to be.

Character data should come from a real source, not my memory

Trouble Brewing I know cold. Across the ~200 official and experimental characters, my recall of every ability, team, setup modifier, jinx, and exact night-order position is good but not perfect. The build will seed character and night-order data from a machine-readable community source (the script-tool JSON format) and use my knowledge as a review pass — not the other way round. All ability wording stays paraphrased or Storyteller-entered; the official text and art never ship in the repo.

The composition table is 5–15 only

The standard table is solid for 5 to 15. Teensyville (5–7) and Travellers (past 15) follow looser, Storyteller-driven conventions, so the app computes a suggestion and always lets the Storyteller edit the final counts.

Waking a locked phone is not reliable

Web push to an installed PWA works, but a phone locked in a pocket for twenty minutes may not ring for the gather signal — iOS especially. Realistic expectation: it's dependable when the app is open or was used recently, and a catch-up prompt shows the moment someone looks. The social layer (people are loosely together at a wedding) carries the rest. This needs real-device testing early.

The join link is the only credential

Join-by-code-and-name means whoever holds a player's link can act as that player. Database row-level security stops cross-player peeking, but the link itself isn't a password. Among wedding guests that's a fair trade; it is not a hardened system.

I can't tell you if it's fun

Phase lengths, whether the meet queue works socially, whether async pacing keeps people engaged — only real games answer that, which is why there's a two-month playtest block. And I can't reproduce venue network conditions from here; a rehearsal on-site is the only real test of the clock.

“Most popular scripts” is a snapshot

The three official scripts and their player counts are stable facts. Community-script popularity shifts and I don't have live rankings — treat any “popular custom script” suggestion as a starting point to check against the current database, not a current chart.

Build roadmap

Sep–Oct

Foundations

Repo, Supabase project, deploy pipeline. Data model. Join flow, seating, the circle with live neighbours. The phase engine and synced clock — proven with three phones on bad wifi.

Nov–Jan

The night

The script engine and character-data import. Night dispatch for all 22 Trouble Brewing characters as the first script. Storyteller night-order walkthrough. Player choice inputs. Auto-computed suggestions for Chef, Empath, Undertaker.

Feb–Mar

The day & the Storyteller

Prep board, meet queue, gather signal, day log, player notebook. First full solo run-through by the Storyteller.

Apr–Jun

Playtest

Real games with friends over video. Fix the clock and reconnection edge cases. Tune phase lengths. Decide whether a second script is worth it.

Jul–Aug

Polish & rehearse

Real token art, sound design, the install prompt. A dress rehearsal at or near the venue. A written run-book for the Storyteller.
